Electronic Market?
>computers matching buyers and sellers instantly.

Key benefits:

· Speed: Trades happen in milliseconds
· Accessibility: Anyone with internet can trade
· Transparency: Everyone can see the same prices and order information
· Lower costs: Less need for middlemen, so fees are smaller

The Order Book

Think of the order book as a giant bulletin board showing:

Buyers Want (Bids) Sellers Want (Asks)
$100.50 (500 shares) $100.75 (300 shares)
$100.25 (1000 shares) $101.00 (800 shares)
$100.00 (2000 shares) $101.25 (500 shares)

· Best Bid: Highest price a buyer is willing to pay ($100.50)
· Best Ask: Lowest price a seller is willing to accept ($100.75)
· Bid-Ask Spread: The gap between them ($0.25)

Why the spread matters:

· Small spread = Easy to trade (liquid market)
· Large spread = Hard to trade (illiquid market)

Types of Trading

Proprietary Trading (Prop Trading)

· The firm trades its own money to make profits
· Like a casino betting with its own money
· Higher risk, but keeps all profits
· Heavily regulated

Agency Trading

· The firm trades on behalf of clients
· Like a travel agent booking flights for you
· Earns commissions/fees, not trading profits
· Must prioritize client's best interests

Order Matching Rules

When a buyer meets a seller, how do we decide who gets priority?

1. Price Priority

· Better prices win
· Higher bids get matched first
· Lower asks get matched first

2. Time Priority

· If prices are equal, earlier orders win
· "First come, first served"

3. Size Priority (less common)

· Larger orders may get priority
· Encourages big orders that add liquidity

The Common Rule: Price/Display/Time

1. Best price first
2. Among same price, displayed orders before hidden orders
3. Among same price and display type, earliest order first

Major Order Types

1. Market Order

"Buy/Sell NOW at whatever price!"

· Executes immediately
· No price guarantee
· Risk: You might pay more than expected (slippage)
· Best for: Highly liquid assets, urgent trades

Example: "Buy 100 shares of Apple at market" → You get whatever the current best price is

---

2. Limit Order

"Buy/Sell ONLY at this price or better"

· You set your price
· May not execute if price never reaches your limit
· No slippage risk
· Best for: Less liquid assets, controlling price

Example: "Buy 100 shares of Apple at $150 or less" → Only executes if price drops to $150

Buy Limit: Set at or below current price
Sell Limit: Set at or above current price

---

3. Stop Order (Stop-Loss)

"Once price hits X, execute a market order"

· Becomes a market order when trigger price is reached
· Used to limit losses or enter positions
· Risk: Slippage after trigger

Example: You own stock at $100. You set a stop-loss at $95. If price drops to $95, it automatically sells at market.

Buy Stop: Placed above current price (for entering long or covering short)
Sell Stop: Placed below current price (for exiting long or entering short)

---

4. Stop-Limit Order

"Once price hits X, place a limit order at Y"

· Combines stop and limit orders
· More control over execution price
· Risk: Order may never fill

Example: "If price drops to $95, place a limit order to sell at $94.50"

---

5. Pegged Order

"Keep my order at a fixed distance from the best price"

· Dynamic limit price that adjusts automatically
· Tracks best bid, best ask, or mid-price
· Useful for spread trading

Example: "Keep my buy order 3 ticks below the best bid"

· If best bid is $100.00, your order is at $99.97
· If best bid moves to $100.50, your order moves to $100.47

---

6. Trailing Stop Order

"Follow the price up, but don't follow it down"

· Stop price adjusts as market moves favorably
· Locks in profits while letting winners run
· Only moves in one direction

Example: You buy at $100 with a $5 trailing stop

· Price rises to $110 → stop moves to $105
· Price rises to $120 → stop moves to $115
· Price falls to $115 → stop triggers, you sell

---

7. Market If Touched (MIT)

"Buy below market / Sell above market"

· Opposite of stop orders
· Buy MIT: Triggers when price falls to your target
· Sell MIT: Triggers when price rises to your target
· Becomes a market order when triggered

Example: Stock at $100. You place a buy MIT at $95. When price drops to $95, it buys at market.

---

8. Fill or Kill (FOK)

"Fill my entire order immediately or cancel it"

· All-or-nothing
· No partial fills
· Used for large orders

---

9. Fill and Kill (FAK)

"Fill what you can immediately, cancel the rest"

· Partial fills allowed
· Remainder cancelled immediately
· More flexible than FOK

---

10. Immediate or Cancel (IOC)

"Execute immediately, cancel any unfilled portion"

· Similar to FAK
· Common in high-frequency trading

Order Flow

Order flow = The net direction of trades

· Buyer-initiated trade: Trade happens at ask price (buyer pays the spread) → Sign = +1
· Seller-initiated trade: Trade happens at bid price (seller pays the spread) → Sign = -1

What it tells us:

· More buy orders than sell orders → Price likely goes UP
· More sell orders than buy orders → Price likely goes DOWN

How to measure:

· Net trade sign: (# buyer trades) - (# seller trades)
· Net trade volume: (buy volume) - (sell volume)

---

Display vs. Non-Display Orders

Display (Lit) Orders

· Visible on the order book
· Higher execution priority
· Cannot "cross the market" (create locked markets)
· Good for traders who want speed

Non-Display (Hidden/Dark) Orders

· Not visible to other traders
· Lower execution priority
· No crossing restrictions
· Good for large institutions hiding their intentions

---

Price Impact

Large market orders move prices!

Example:

· You want to buy 10,000 shares
· Best ask: $100 for 5,000 shares
· Next ask: $100.02 for 5,000 shares
· Your average price: $100.01 (not $100!)

Solutions:

· Use limit orders
· Use iceberg orders (hide total size)
· Break orders into smaller pieces
