Forward and Futures Contracts - Simple Explanation

What Are Forward and Futures Contracts?

Imagine you're a farmer growing wheat. You worry: "What if wheat prices drop before harvest?" A baker worries: "What if wheat prices rise before I need to buy?"

Solution: You both agree TODAY on a price for wheat to be delivered LATER.

That's a forward/futures contract — an agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a future date.

Simple Example

 Farmer (Seller) Baker (Buyer)
Worry Price drops Price rises
Action Agrees to sell at $5/bushel Agrees to buy at $5/bushel
Result Knows income in advance Knows cost in advance

Both sides lock in the price and remove uncertainty.

---

Forward vs. Futures: Key Differences

Feature Forward Contract Futures Contract
Where traded Over-the-counter (private) Regulated exchange
Customization Fully customizable Standardized
Counterparty risk High (no guarantee) Low (clearing house guarantees)
Liquidity Low (hard to exit) High (easy to trade)
Settlement Usually physical delivery Physical or cash
Margin Usually none Required
Regulation Light Heavy

Simple analogy:

· Forward = Custom-made suit (perfect fit, but hard to sell to someone else)
· Futures = Off-the-rack suit (standard sizes, easy to buy/sell anytime)

---

The Four Parameters of a Futures Contract

1. Lot Size

How much of the underlying asset is in one contract.

· Example: Apple futures lot size = 100 shares
· You can only trade in multiples of 100

2. Contract Value

Total value = Lot Size × Current Price

· Example: 100 shares × $125 = $12,500

3. Margin

The deposit you need to open a position.

· Initial Margin: What you deposit to open (e.g., $1,000)
· Maintenance Margin: Minimum you must keep (e.g., $900)
· If your account drops below maintenance, you get a margin call

4. Expiration Date

When the contract ends and settlement occurs.

---

Why Trade Futures? Two Main Reasons

Hedging (Reducing Risk)

Who: Producers and consumers of goods
Goal: Lock in prices to avoid uncertainty

Examples:

· Airline hedges fuel costs by buying oil futures
· Farmer hedges crop prices by selling wheat futures
· Manufacturer hedges raw material costs

Think of it like: Buying insurance for your business

Speculation (Seeking Profit)

Who: Traders and investors
Goal: Profit from price movements

Examples:

· Trader bets oil will rise, buys oil futures
· Fund manager bets gold will fall, sells gold futures

Think of it like: Betting on which way prices will go

Key point: Speculators provide liquidity, making it easier for hedgers to trade.

---

Settlement: How Contracts End

Physical Delivery

Actually exchange the real asset.

· Buyer pays the agreed price
· Seller delivers the goods (oil, wheat, gold, etc.)

Example: You bought oil futures at $60/barrel. At expiration, you pay $60,000 and receive 1,000 real barrels of oil.

Cash Settlement

No physical exchange — just transfer the profit/loss.

· If price rose: Seller pays buyer the difference
· If price fell: Buyer pays seller the difference

Example: Oil futures at $60, spot price at $65. Seller pays buyer $5,000 (1,000 barrels × $5).

Most Traders Don't Hold to Expiration

They close out before expiry by:

· Buying an offsetting contract (if short)
· Selling an offsetting contract (if long)

This avoids the hassle of delivery.

---

Leverage: The Double-Edged Sword

Leverage = Controlling a large position with a small deposit.

Example:

· Contract value: $12,500 (100 shares × $125)
· Initial margin: $1,000
· Leverage: 12.5x

If price rises to $140:

· Profit: $1,500
· Return on $1,000 margin: 150%

If price falls to $110:

· Loss: $1,500
· You lose more than your initial margin!

Key insight: Leverage amplifies both gains AND losses.

---

The Clearing House: The Middleman

Problem: How do you know the other party will honor the contract?

Solution: The clearing house steps in as middleman.

```
Buyer ←→ Clearing House ←→ Seller
```

What the clearing house does:

1. Becomes buyer to every seller
2. Becomes seller to every buyer
3. Guarantees all obligations
4. Collects and monitors margin
5. Matches and confirms trades

Result: No counterparty risk for traders.

---

Mark-to-Market: Daily Settlement

Every day, the exchange adjusts your account to reflect current prices.

Example:

Day Price Long Trader Short Trader
Start $100 $100 margin $100 margin
Day 1 $105 +$5 → $105 -$5 → $95
Day 2 $85 -$20 → $85 +$20 → $115
Day 2 action  Margin call: deposit $15 → $100 Keep $115

Why this matters:

· Prevents large losses from accumulating
· Ensures traders can meet obligations
· Money moves daily from losers to winners

Pricing Forward Contracts

The Formula

F = S \times e^{r(T-t)}

Where:

· F = Forward price
· S = Current spot price
· r = Risk-free interest rate
· T-t = Time until expiration

The Logic (No-Arbitrage)

Key idea: Two ways to get the same asset should cost the same.

Method 1: Buy forward contract

· Pay F at time T

Method 2: Buy asset now with borrowed money

· Borrow S at time t
· Repay S × e^(r(T-t)) at time T

For no arbitrage: F = S × e^(r(T-t))

Arbitrage Examples

If F > S × e^(r(T-t)) (forward overpriced):

1. Borrow S, buy asset
2. Short forward contract
3. At delivery: sell asset for F, repay loan
4. Profit: F - S × e^(r(T-t))

If F < S × e^(r(T-t)) (forward underpriced):

1. Short asset, invest proceeds
2. Long forward contract
3. At delivery: buy asset for F, close short
4. Profit: S × e^(r(T-t)) - F

---

Pricing Futures Contracts

The Formula

F = S \times e^{(r + s - c)(T-t)}

Where:

· r = Risk-free rate
· s = Storage cost (%)
· c = Convenience yield (%)

Components

Component Effect on Futures Price Why
Spot price Base Starting point
Interest rate Increases Opportunity cost of money
Storage cost Increases Cost to hold asset
Convenience yield Decreases Benefit of holding asset

Convenience Yield Explained

What is it? The benefit of actually holding the asset.

Examples:

· Holding stocks → earn dividends
· Holding currencies → earn interest
· Holding commodities → benefit from shortages

---

Contango and Backwardation

Contango

Futures price > Current spot price

Normal situation for:

· Assets with storage costs
· Markets expecting price rises

Example: Oil at $80 spot, 3-month futures at $85

Backwardation

Futures price < Current spot price

Happens when:

· Convenience yield is high
· Markets expect price declines
· Current supply is tight

Example: Oil at $80 spot, 3-month futures at $75

Practical Example: Oil Futures

Scenario: You enter a long oil futures contract at $60/barrel, 1,000 barrels.

If spot price at expiry is $58:

· You lose: ($60 - $58) × 1,000 = $2,000
· Short trader gains: $2,000

If spot price at expiry is $65:

· You gain: ($65 - $60) × 1,000 = $5,000
· Short trader loses: $5,000

Key insight: Futures is a zero-sum game — one party's gain is the other's loss.

Common Technical Indicators (for Futures Trading)

RSI (Relative Strength Index)

· Measures momentum (0-100)
· Above 70 = overbought (may fall)
· Below 30 = oversold (may rise)

Bollinger Bands

· Middle line = moving average
· Upper/lower bands = ± standard deviations
· Wide bands = high volatility
· Narrow bands = low volatility

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)

· MACD line crosses above signal = buy signal
· MACD line crosses below signal = sell signal
· Above zero = upward momentum
· Below zero = downward momentum

The Big Picture

Why forwards/futures matter:

1. Risk management — Lock in prices, reduce uncertainty
2. Price discovery — Market expectations of future prices
3. Liquidity — Easy to enter/exit positions
4. Leverage — Control large positions with little capital
5. Flexibility — Hedge or speculate, your choice

Remember:

· Forwards = private, customizable, higher risk
· Futures = public, standardized, lower risk
· Both let you fix prices for the future
· Margin and mark-to-market protect the system
· Contango/backwardation tell you about market expectations
